New York Paycheck Calculator 2026 — How Much Is $80K a Year After Taxes?

The offer letter says $80,000 in New York. You read it twice, then start doing the thing everyone does: dividing by 12 and imagining what actually lands in the bank on payday.

Then you remember the city part. New York is not one tax situation. It is at least two, and the one you are in depends on which side of the city line you sleep on.

Quick answer: A single filer earning $80,000 who lives in New York City, with no 401(k), HSA, or health premiums, has a 2026 US Salary Tools estimate of $58,375.51 annual take-home pay — roughly 73% of gross. Living in New York State but outside the five boroughs raises that to $61,041.40. The same $80,000 in Texas or Florida comes to $65,110.00.

One thing to get straight before the tables. Phrases people type into Google — “NY paycheck calculator”, “$80k after taxes NYC” — sound like they return a fact. They return an estimate. This page models one clean scenario: tax year 2026, gross $80,000.00, single, NYC resident, no pre-tax benefits. It is a US Salary Tools engine output, not a payroll stub and not an IRS, New York State, or New York City determination.

Is $80,000 in New York the same as $80,000 in Texas?

No, and the gap is not small.

Texas and Florida have no individual state income tax, so the only mandatory bites are federal. New York layers on a state personal income tax (PIT) and a Paid Family Leave payroll contribution. If you live in the city, a New York City resident income tax stacks on top of that.

An honest limitation: Yonkers is not modeled in this calculator. Yonkers runs its own resident surcharge and nonresident earnings tax in the real world, and this engine produces no Yonkers line. How the federal slice is calculated

The federal portion here is identical to the California, Texas, and Florida guides. The federal government does not care which state you live in for this line.

The standard deduction is the flat amount the IRS lets you subtract before brackets apply, so you do not have to itemize receipts. For a single filer in 2026 the site uses $16,100. What remains runs through progressive federal brackets, where each slice is taxed at its own rate — your top bracket never applies to your whole salary.

For this example, the federal income tax comes to $8,770.00, about 11.0% of gross for that line alone.

FICA still applies in every state

Social Security and Medicare, together called FICA, are charged on gross wages.

Social Security is 6.2% of $80,000, or $4,960.00. An $80,000 salary sits well under the 2026 Social Security wage base, so every dollar is taxed. Medicare is 1.45% with no cap, or $1,160.00. These two lines are identical in Manhattan, Rochester, Houston, and Miami.

New York state income tax — it is not a no-tax state

Some paycheck content treats New York as if the city tax were the whole story. It is not. The state PIT is the larger of the two New York income-tax lines in this example.

New York uses a progressive schedule, same idea as the federal system: rising rates applied to successive slices of taxable income. Before those rates apply, the engine subtracts New York's own standard deduction, which is a different number from the federal one.

NYC resident income tax

New York City levies its own personal income tax on residents, administered through the state return rather than a separate city filing.

In the calculator this line appears only when two conditions are both true: local jurisdiction is NYC and residency is Resident. On $80,000 the result is $2,665.89 single and $2,268.86 married filing jointly.

NYC nonresidents

If you commute in from New Jersey, Connecticut, Long Island, or Westchester, this calculator charges you $0.00 of NYC local income tax. NYC PIT is modeled as resident-only.

That is the tool's rule, not a universal promise about every New York filing situation. Residency for tax purposes can hinge on domicile, days spent in the city, and part-year moves. If your status is genuinely unclear, that is a question for New York State or a tax professional, not a calculator.

NY Paid Family Leave is not income tax

PFL is the line most people have never heard of until they see it on a stub. It is not a tax on income in the ordinary sense — it is an employee contribution funding New York's paid family leave insurance benefit.

The engine applies 0.432% of gross wages up to a wage base of $91,514.82. An $80,000 salary is entirely below that base, so the full salary is subject to the rate and the deduction is $345.60 for the year.

That is the only New York payroll line in this model. The engine does not add a separate state disability or SDI deduction to this example, so do not expect one in the tables above.

Why your actual New York paycheck will differ

Every figure above is an annual estimate produced by a model. Your stub is produced by a payroll system following your employer's settings and your own paperwork. They rarely match exactly.

Withholding is not your annual liability. Your employer sends estimated prepayments to the IRS and New York every payday based on your Form W-4 and IT-2104. The gap between what was withheld and what you actually owe becomes your refund or balance due at filing.

Extra withholding. Any additional amount you requested on your W-4 or IT-2104 shrinks each check without changing what you owe.

Pre-tax benefits. A traditional 401(k), an HSA, and pre-tax health premiums all reduce taxable wages. This scenario assumes none, which is why it sits at the high end of a real New York paycheck.

City status. Moving across the city line mid-year, or being treated as a part-year resident, splits your year into two tax pictures. These tables assume a full year in one status.

Unmodeled locals. Yonkers and other local taxes are not in this engine, as noted above.

Bonuses, commissions, and second jobs. Supplemental wages are often withheld at flat rates that differ from regular-wage withholding. If overtime is a regular part of your pay, see the no tax on overtime 2026 explainer.
