Washington Paycheck Calculator 2026 — How Much Is $80K a Year After Taxes?

A single filer earning $80,000 in Washington in 2026 keeps an estimated $64,000.27 a year in this calculator — not the $65,110.00 that a no-income-tax state like Texas produces on the same salary. Washington charges $0.00 of wage income tax, so the entire difference comes from two state payroll deductions that are not income tax at all: WA PFML (paid family and medical leave) and WA Cares (long-term care).

That is the whole story of the Washington paycheck in one sentence, and it is also the part most “no income tax state” lists get wrong. Washington belongs on that list. It still takes something off your stub.

Breakdown · vs Texas · PFML · WA Cares · 2028 tax · FAQ

Quick answer: On $80,000 gross, single, no 401(k), HSA, or health premiums, the 2026 US Salary Tools estimate for Washington is $64,000.27 of annual take-home pay — about 80% of gross. The same $80,000 in Texas or Florida comes to $65,110.00. The $1,109.73 gap is WA PFML plus WA Cares, not state income tax.

One caveat before the tables. Searches like “Washington paycheck calculator” or “$80,000 after taxes in Washington” look like they return a fact. They return an estimate. This page models one fixed scenario: tax year 2026, gross $80,000.00, single, Washington, no local income tax, no pre-tax benefits. It is a US Salary Tools engine output — not a payroll stub, and not an IRS, ESD, or WA Cares Fund determination.

Is $80,000 in Washington the same as $80,000 in Texas?

Almost. Not quite.

Texas and Florida charge no individual income tax on wages, and in this engine they also carry no employee-side state payroll deduction. That is why both land on exactly the same number. Washington also charges no wage income tax in 2026 — but the state runs two employee-funded insurance programs that come out of gross pay, so the Washington stub is thinner.

Also, two words payroll teams use loosely. Semimonthly means twice a month — 24 checks. Biweekly means every other Friday — 26 checks, each smaller, same annual total. Two extra checks a year is not extra money.

No 2026 wage income tax — the 2028 schedule and Initiative 645

Washington has no wage income tax in tax year 2026. This $80,000 paycheck includes $0.00 of Washington income tax. Every line above holds that position, and nothing in the current legislative picture changes it for this year.

What has changed is the forward calendar, and it is worth getting the wording exactly right because the internet has already garbled it.

ESSB 6346 enacted a 9.9% tax on Washington taxable income above a $1 million standard deduction, scheduled to begin January 1, 2028. Read the threshold carefully: the first $1 million of taxable income is deducted before the rate applies. An $80,000 salary is not within an order of magnitude of that floor.

Initiative 645 is certified for the November 3, 2026 statewide ballot and, if approved, would repeal that tax before 2028. A court challenge to the tax also exists, running on its own track.

So there are three live possibilities for 2028 and beyond: the tax takes effect as scheduled, voters repeal it, or litigation alters it. This page does not predict which. Either outcome leaves this $80,000 2026 example unchanged — the 9.9% rate is not applied to $80,000 here, and no Washington income tax line exists on a 2026 wage stub in this engine.

One more separation. Washington’s capital gains excise tax is a distinct tax on certain long-term gains, filed separately. It is not a wage withholding line and does not appear in the breakdown above.

And at the city level: Seattle’s JumpStart payroll expense tax is levied on the employer for high-compensation payroll, and it legally cannot be withheld from an employee’s wages — it is $0.00 on this stub and is not in the breakdown table.

WA PFML is not income tax

WA PFML — Washington Paid Family and Medical Leave — is the larger of the two state deductions on this paycheck, and it is the one people most often misread as a stealth income tax. It is not. It is an insurance premium. It funds the program that pays you partial wage replacement while you bond with a new child, recover from a serious health condition, or care for a family member who has one.

Here is how the 2026 rate actually stacks up, since this is where most published figures go wrong.

Component2026 value

Wage cap$184,500 (Social Security wage base)
Employee share of total premium71.43%

Gross wages subject to premium$80,000.00
WA PFML withheld in this engine$645.73

The 1.13% is the total premium, not the employee rate. ESD publishes the combined number for 2026. The employee portion is 71.43% of that total, which works out to an effective employee rate of 0.807159% of gross. Applying 1.13% directly to your salary overstates the deduction; applying a rough 0.71% understates it. This engine withholds $645.73.

The wage cap is the Social Security wage base. At $184,500 for 2026, an $80,000 salary is entirely below the cap, so the full salary is subject to the premium and nothing is excluded.

Employer size changes who pays the rest, not your line. Employers with 50 or more employees pay the other 28.57% of the total premium. Small employers may skip the employer share entirely — the employee share still comes out of employee wages, so this example uses $645.73 either way. Some employers voluntarily cover part of the employee share; if yours does, your real deduction is lower. Employers may also run an approved voluntary plan instead of the state program. That variation is not modeled here.

WA Cares

WA Cares is the simpler of the two deductions. It funds Washington’s long-term care benefit — in-home care, equipment, and related support later in life, the kind of cost Medicare largely does not cover.

The mechanics are flat: 0.58% of gross wages. On $80,000, that is $464.00 a year.

Note the cap row, because it is the one structural difference from PFML. WA Cares is not capped at the Social Security wage base in this engine. Every dollar of covered wages is subject to the 0.58%. That distinction is invisible at $80,000 — you are under the PFML cap anyway — but it matters above $184,500, where PFML stops and WA Cares keeps going.

Exemptions do exist in real life. Certain workers can apply for a permanent or conditional exemption. This scenario assumes the premium is withheld. With an approved exemption, subtract $464.00 and take-home rises accordingly.

The federal slice and FICA

With no state income tax to calculate, the federal portion is essentially the entire income-tax story on a Washington paycheck. It is also identical to the California, Texas, Florida, and New York guides — the IRS does not care which state you live in.

The joint walk is short: a $32,200 standard deduction leaves $47,800 of taxable income, taxed at 10% on the first $24,800 ($2,480.00) and 12% on the next $23,000 ($2,760.00), for $5,240.00 of federal income tax. Joint annual take-home is $67,530.27, or $5,627.52 a month.

Notice what does not move. Social Security, Medicare, WA PFML, and WA Cares are percentage-of-wages charges; filing status is irrelevant to all four. And the federal line is never zero on $80,000 — $5,240.00 joint is a real bill, not a rounding artifact.

The standing caveat: this joint scenario assumes no additional spouse wages. A second income changes the household picture substantially and usually pushes taxable income back into higher brackets.

Why your actual Washington paycheck will differ

Every figure above is an annual estimate produced by a model. Your stub is produced by a payroll system following your employer’s configuration and your own paperwork. They rarely match to the cent.

  • WA Cares exemptions. An approved exemption removes the $464.00 line entirely. This scenario assumes no exemption.
  • PFML voluntary plans. If your employer runs an approved voluntary plan instead of the state program, the premium structure on your stub can differ from the ESD schedule modeled here.
  • Employer coverage of the employee share. Some Washington employers pay part or all of the employee PFML premium as a benefit. That reduces or eliminates the $645.73 line for you, even though the program cost has not changed.
  • Withholding is not your annual liability. Your employer sends estimated federal prepayments every payday based on your Form W-4. The gap between what was withheld and what you actually owe becomes your refund or balance due at filing.
  • Pre-tax benefits. A traditional 401(k), an HSA, and pre-tax health premiums all reduce federal taxable wages. This scenario assumes none.
  • Bonuses, commissions, and second jobs. Supplemental wages are often withheld at flat rates that differ from regular-wage withholding. If overtime is regular in your pay, see the no tax on overtime 2026 explainer.
  • Mid-year moves. Relocating into or out of Washington splits your year across two state tax pictures.

To see how each component is built, read the Methodology. For gross-pay conversions, use the salary calculator.

How to run this in the paycheck calculator

Reproducing the headline number takes under a minute.

  1. Open the paycheck calculator.
  2. Enter a salary of 80000.
  3. Choose your pay frequency — weekly, biweekly, semimonthly, or monthly.
  4. Set state to Washington.
  5. Leave the local jurisdiction field empty — Washington has no local income tax in this engine.
  6. Set filing status to Single, and leave 401(k), HSA, and health premium fields empty.
  7. Press Calculate. Annual take-home should show $64,000.27.

If your result differs, the usual culprits are a stray benefit amount left in a field from a previous run, or a filing status other than Single. Switch the status to Married Filing Jointly and the annual figure should move to $67,530.27.
