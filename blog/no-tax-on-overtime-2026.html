No Tax on Overtime 2026: How the New IRS Deduction Works and How Much You Save

You worked 55 hours last week, saw the extra money on your check, and then watched taxes eat a chunk of it. So where is the "no tax on overtime" everyone talked about?

It is real, but the name is misleading. The no tax on overtime 2026 rule is a deduction you claim when you file — not a payroll exemption that makes your check bigger.

Quick answer: There is no full tax exemption. For 2026 you can deduct up to $12,500 of qualified overtime premium pay ($25,000 joint) on your tax return. A single filer in the 22% bracket with $7,500 of overtime premium saves about $1,650. Payroll taxes still apply.

Key takeaways

  • Only the premium half of time-and-a-half counts — not your whole overtime check.
  • The cap is $12,500 per return, or $25,000 if married filing jointly.
  • The deduction shrinks once your income passes $150,000 ($300,000 joint) and disappears entirely at $275,000 single.
  • Social Security and Medicare taxes still hit every dollar of overtime.
  • You get the money as a smaller tax bill or bigger refund — unless you update your Form W-4.

Is overtime tax-free in 2026?

No. Overtime is still fully taxable wages.

According to the IRS, the deduction "does not mean that overtime compensation is excluded or exempt from gross income." Overtime stays subject to income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare, and federal unemployment tax.

What the 2025 tax law actually created is a new overtime tax deduction that runs from tax year 2025 through 2028. It lowers your taxable income at filing time. It does not remove tax from your paycheck.

Two terms worth separating here:

  • Withholding — the estimated prepayment your employer sends to the IRS every payday. It is not your final tax.
  • Tax liability — what you actually owe after you file. The gap between the two is your refund or your balance due.

The overtime deduction reduces your liability. Your withholding stays the same unless you change it yourself.

What counts as "qualified overtime" for the deduction?

This is where most people overestimate their benefit.

Only the premium portion qualifies — the extra "half" in time-and-a-half. The straight-time pay for those same hours does not count.

Say you earn $30 an hour and work 10 overtime hours in a week:

  • You get paid $45 per overtime hour, so $450 total.
  • $300 of that is straight time (10 × $30). Not deductible.
  • $150 is the premium (10 × $15). That is your qualified overtime compensation.

Three more rules decide whether your hours count at all:

It must be required by federal law

Qualified overtime is overtime required under section 7 of the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) — generally 1.5× your regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. The workweek is a fixed 7-day period, and each week stands alone. You cannot average two weeks together.

Extra-generous overtime does not fully count

If your employer pays double time, only the amount needed to satisfy the federal 1.5× requirement is qualified. The IRS uses this example: an employee paid $40/hour for overtime at a $20 regular rate has $400 of overtime pay for 10 hours, but only $100 counts as qualified overtime compensation.

The same logic applies to overtime that only your state requires. California's daily overtime rules, for instance, go beyond the federal weekly test. Premiums paid purely under state law or a union contract are not qualified overtime for this federal deduction. Check your state labor agency for your daily overtime rights — they still apply, they just do not feed this tax break.

You must be FLSA overtime-eligible

Salaried executive, administrative, and professional employees are commonly exempt from FLSA overtime, along with outside sales staff, certain computer-related roles, and some transportation workers. If you are exempt, you do not have qualified overtime compensation even if your employer pays you extra for long weeks.

How much does the no tax on overtime 2026 deduction save you?

A deduction is not a refund. It cuts your taxable income, so your savings equal the deduction multiplied by your marginal rate — the rate on your next dollar of income.

Here is a full worked example.

Assumptions: single filer, $30/hour, 40 regular hours plus 10 overtime hours per week, 50 weeks worked, standard deduction, tax year 2026. This example assumes no 401(k), health insurance, or other pre-tax deductions, and no state income tax.

Gross pay$82,500

The deduction is worth about $1,650 a year here. Notice the FICA rows do not move at all.

Here is the bracket math, one layer at a time, using the 2026 rates. Without the deduction, on $66,400 of taxable income:

  • First $12,400 at 10% = $1,240
  • $12,400 to $50,400 → $38,000 at 12% = $4,560
  • $50,400 to $66,400 → $16,000 at 22% = $3,520
  • Total = $9,320

With the deduction, on $58,900:

  • First $12,400 at 10% = $1,240
  • $12,400 to $50,400 → $38,000 at 12% = $4,560
  • $50,400 to $58,900 → $8,500 at 22% = $1,870
  • Total = $7,670

The whole $7,500 deduction came off the top, where income was taxed at 22%. So $7,500 × 22% = $1,650.

Worth noting: this worker's marginal rate is 22%, but the effective rate — total federal income tax plus FICA divided by gross pay — is about 16.9%. Your top bracket never applies to all your income.

What happens if you earn more than $150,000?

The deduction phases out above $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income (MAGI), or $300,000 for joint filers. MAGI here is your adjusted gross income plus certain foreign and territory income you excluded.

The formula on Schedule 1-A is mechanical: for every full $1,000 your MAGI exceeds the threshold, the deduction drops by $100.

MAGI (single filer)Deduction on $12,500 premium
$150,000 or less$12,500
$175,000$10,000
$200,000$7,500
$250,000$2,500
$275,000 or more$0

A single filer at $175,000 MAGI is $25,000 over the line, so the deduction drops by 25 × $100 = $2,500.

For joint filers, the same $100-per-$1,000 reduction applies above $300,000, and the $25,000 deduction reaches zero at $550,000.

Why your paycheck will not suddenly grow

This surprises a lot of workers. The IRS is explicit: an employer may not reduce income tax withholding to account for this deduction on its own.

So your overtime is still withheld on as normal wages. You collect the benefit at filing time.

If you would rather have the money during the year, the 2026 Form W-4 was updated for exactly this. You can estimate your expected overtime deduction in Step 4(b) using the deductions worksheet, and your employer will withhold less. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator was also updated to handle it.

One caution: guess too high on your overtime hours and you under-withhold, which means owing money in April. If your overtime is unpredictable, taking the refund is the safer route.

How you claim it, and what your W-2 must show

Starting with tax year 2026, your employer is required to report qualified overtime compensation separately on your Form W-2 in Box 12, code TT. Independent-contractor style reporting uses Form 1099-MISC box 14 or Form 1099-NEC box 1d, but that is rare.

You then complete Part III of Schedule 1-A and carry the result to Form 1040 as an additional deduction. It works whether you itemize or take the standard deduction.

Three requirements are easy to trip over:

  • You need a Social Security number valid for employment, listed on the return.
  • If you are married, you must file jointly. Filing separately disqualifies you.
  • The amount must actually appear on a properly furnished W-2. The IRS says if your employer omitted or understated it, you must request a Form W-2c. Without the corrected form, you cannot claim the missing amount — even if you genuinely earned it.

Box 12 code TT shows your total qualified overtime, not your deductible amount. If it says $30,000, you still cap out at $12,500 on the return.

Sources: IRS, Working Families Tax Cuts — individuals and workers; IRS, FS-2026-13, updated Q&As on the deduction for qualified overtime compensation (August 2026); IRS, Schedule 1-A, Additional Deductions (FS-2026-04); SSA, 2026 Social Security changes fact sheet; IRS, Additional Medicare Tax thresholds.

What about payroll taxes and state tax?

Two things the overtime deduction does not touch.

FICA still applies to every overtime dollar

Social Security and Medicare — together called FICA — are charged on your gross wages, not your taxable income. The standard deduction and the overtime deduction do nothing to them.

For 2026, per the SSA: Social Security is 6.2% on wages up to a $184,500 wage base, and Medicare is 1.45% with no cap. An extra 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax applies to wages above $200,000 single, $250,000 married filing jointly, or $125,000 married filing separately. That 0.9% is employee-only — there is no employer match on it, and employers start withholding it above $200,000 regardless of your filing status. That mismatch is why some higher earners see a surprise refund or balance due.

State income tax is a separate system

The federal deduction is claimed after adjusted gross income, not before it. That matters, because most states build their return starting from your federal AGI — so the deduction usually does not carry over automatically.

Some states passed their own conformity or decoupling rules after the 2025 federal law, and a handful start from federal taxable income instead of AGI. The only reliable answer is your own state's revenue agency for the 2026 tax year.

If you live in a state with no broad wage income tax, this is moot — you already had no state withholding on that overtime.
