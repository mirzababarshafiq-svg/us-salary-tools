California Paycheck Calculator 2026 — How Much Is $80K a Year After Taxes?

You earn $80,000 a year in California. Your gross salary is $6,666.67 per month, but your take-home pay is lower because several separate taxes and payroll deductions apply.

For a W-2 employee, the major layers in this example are federal income tax, California income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and California State Disability Insurance (SDI). Your filing status and voluntary pre-tax benefits can change the final result.

Quick answer: For a single employee earning $80,000 with no voluntary pre-tax deductions, the 2026 US Salary Tools estimate is $60,706.58 annual take-home pay, or $5,058.88 per month.

What taxes come out of an $80,000 California paycheck?

The example includes $8,770.00 of federal income tax, $3,363.42 of California income tax, $4,960.00 of Social Security, $1,160.00 of Medicare, and $1,040.00 of California SDI.

Total Social Security plus Medicare is $6,120.00. SDI is a separate California payroll deduction and should not be confused with California personal income tax.

Federal taxable income on $80,000

For a single filer in this example, the federal standard deduction is $16,100. Subtracting that from $80,000 leaves $63,900 of federal taxable income before other adjustments.

The modeled federal income tax on that taxable amount is $8,770.00. The effective federal income-tax amount relative to the $80,000 gross salary is about 11.0%.

California income tax is progressive

California does not apply one flat income-tax percentage to your entire salary. The state uses a progressive personal income-tax structure in which different portions of taxable income can fall into different marginal brackets.

That is why a simple calculation such as “$80,000 times one California rate” can give a misleading answer. The engine uses the modeled filing-status schedule instead of treating the entire salary as one flat-tax base.

California SDI is separate from income tax

California State Disability Insurance is a payroll withholding separate from personal income tax.

The 2026 rate used in this example is 1.3%. On $80,000 of wages, that is $1,040.00.

Important: When comparing take-home pay, keep California SDI separate from California income tax so the deduction rows reconcile correctly.

Social Security and Medicare on $80,000

Social Security is $4,960.00 in the example and Medicare is $1,160.00. Combined FICA is therefore $6,120.00.

These are payroll taxes, not the same thing as federal income tax. A salary calculator should show them separately when explaining how gross wages turn into take-home pay.

California single vs. married filing jointly

Filing status changes the federal tax result and can also affect state tax. At the same $80,000 gross salary, the modeled federal income tax is different for a married filing jointly return.

How voluntary deductions change take-home pay

A traditional 401(k), HSA contribution, or employer health premium can reduce the amount of cash reaching your bank account while also changing the tax base used in the calculation.

For example, the calculator supports a traditional 401(k) percentage. When you add a contribution, the result separates the pre-tax deduction from the remaining taxes so you can see the difference between saving money and paying taxes.

A salary calculator answers questions such as annual, monthly, weekly, and hourly equivalents. A paycheck calculator focuses on what may reach your bank account after modeled taxes and deductions.

How to compare California take-home pay with another state

Holding salary constant is useful when comparing locations. An $80,000 offer can lead to different take-home amounts because state income taxes and state payroll deductions vary.

Use the State Tax Comparison Calculator to compare two states. For another $80,000 example, see the Texas Paycheck Calculator 2026 guide.

Common mistakes when estimating California take-home pay

  • Using one flat California percentage. California income tax is progressive.
  • Ignoring SDI. SDI is a separate payroll deduction.
  • Confusing gross salary with taxable income. Deductions and benefits can change the taxable amount.
  • Ignoring filing status. Federal and state calculations can differ by filing status.
  • Assuming every paycheck equals annual net divided by 12. Payroll systems can apply period-specific withholding and rounding.
